Usability is not decoration. It is not guessing. And it is definitely not just making things look nice.
Good usability means that real people can use a product, understand it, trust it, and achieve their goals without friction.
Here are ten usability slogans every product team should remember. Jakob Nielsen wrote them down in 1993, in his book Usability Engineering. They are still true today.
One: Your best guess is not good enough. Designers, developers, founders, and stakeholders all have opinions. But opinions are not evidence. Your best guess may be smart. It may be experienced. It may even be correct sometimes. Test early. Observe carefully. And let behavior replace assumptions.
Two: The user is always right. The user is always right about their experience. When users are confused, they are confused. When they cannot find something, they cannot find it. When they feel frustrated, that frustration is real. We should not argue with user behavior. A user who fails at a task is not the problem. The interface is giving us feedback.
Three: The user is not always right. But users are not always right about the solution. They can tell us what hurts. They can show us where they struggle. They can explain what they expected. But they may not know the best design pattern, the technical constraints, or the long-term product strategy. Listen to users deeply. But do not simply copy every suggestion. Users reveal problems. Designers create solutions.
Four: Users are not designers. Users should not have to design the product for us. Their job is to use the system. Our job is to understand their needs, their context, their mental models, and their pain points. Do not ask only what they want. Watch what they do.
Five: Designers are not users. Designers know too much. They know where the button is. They know what the icon means. They know why the workflow was built that way. That knowledge makes them biased. The interface that feels obvious to the team may feel completely unclear to someone seeing it for the first time. So never use the product team as the only test group. Designers design for users. They are not the users.
Six: Vice presidents are not users. Stakeholders matter. Business goals matter. Leadership matters. But executives are rarely the people using the product every day. A vice president may have a strong opinion, but that opinion is not a substitute for user research. The best product decisions balance business needs with real user evidence. A loud voice in the room should not replace the quiet truth from usability testing.
Seven: Less is more. Every extra option adds cognitive load. Every unnecessary field slows people down. Every additional feature competes for attention. Less is more does not mean empty. It means focused. Remove what distracts. Simplify what confuses. Prioritize what matters. The best interface often feels invisible, because it helps users move forward without effort.
Eight: Details matter. Usability lives in the details. Button labels. Error messages. Spacing. Loading states. Form validation. Keyboard navigation. Microcopy. Small details create trust. Small details prevent mistakes. Small details make a product feel professional. A confusing label can break a workflow. A clear message can save it. Details are not decoration. Details are usability.
Nine: Help doesn't. Help pages, tooltips, and documentation can be useful. But they should not be used to repair a bad interface. If users constantly need help, the product is probably too confusing. Good usability means the interface explains itself, through structure, language, feedback, and clear interaction. Help should support the experience. It should not be required to survive it.
Ten: Usability engineering is a process. Usability is not a one-time review. It is a process. Research. Prototype. Test. Measure. Improve. Repeat. Great usability comes from iteration. You do not guess once and hope forever. You build, observe, learn, and refine. Usability engineering turns design from opinion into evidence.
These slogans are simple. But they are powerful. Better usability equals better products.
Do not rely on guesses. Respect users, but do not make them designers. Listen to evidence, not hierarchy. Simplify. Care about details. And treat usability as an ongoing process.
Because great products are not just built. They are tested, learned from, and improved.
Usability is not magic. It is method.
